**LEAP AND TAKE! SWEEP WHOLE LINES AWAY! AND DON'T TOSS AWAY THE KINGDOM!**

Twenty-five points, twelve men a side, and one empty point in the middle. Alquerque is war on a lined board: your men leap the enemy like draughts pieces, and leap again, and again, and every man they jump comes off.

Then cross to Madagascar, where the board doubles to forty-five points, the armies grow to twenty-two men each, and fanorona does something no other game does. You capture by moving towards an enemy, or away from one, and the whole unbroken line of enemies in that direction is swept off the board at once! One man can run amok through the enemy lines, turning at every step, taking a line here and a line there, as long as he finds foes to eat. Just don't eat at both ends, like a leech: the Malagasy proverb forbids it, and so do the rules.

Lost a game? Then comes the vela! The winner hands you seventeen of his men, one at a time, without taking a single one of yours, and only then turns on you with the five he has left. In olden times, a missionary heard, the loser was forgiven only after kneeling before the victor and bleating like a sheep. You are spared that part.

- **Alquerque de doze, after Alfonso X**: twelve men each, as a king of Castile had it written down in 1283; take when you like, stop when you like
- **Alquerque after Bell**: the hard school, where taking is compulsory and no man ever steps backwards or comes back to a point he has stood on
- **Fanorona, today's rules**: approach and withdrawal on the board of nine, and a capture must be made when one can be
- **Fanorona after Montgomery**: the rules as they were first written down, in 1886, beside a whole game "of average native skill"
- **The vela**: the game of revenge, seventeen men handed over before the winner strikes back
- **Fanoron-dimy**: "a small, quick fanorona" on five by five
- **The computer at three strengths**, from either side, or a friend at the same keyboard

## Controls

- **Arrows**: move the cursor; **Enter** or **Space**: pick up a man, then put it on a lit point; a turn that takes again goes on from where the man stands; where one step could take two lines, point at the line you want swept away; **Esc**: let go of a man, or stop taking where the rules allow
- **Mouse**: click
- The men a step would take are marked ✕ when the cursor is on where it goes
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

Alfonso X of Castile, called the Wise, had his Book of Games made in Seville in 1283: chess, dice and tables, and a part of its own for the games on lined boards, the morris games, a hare hunt and alquerque of twelve, its name from the Arabic al-qirq. Those few lines are the oldest rules of the game anyone has found. The book's pictures show the board edged in dark and white woods, with gold quatrefoils holding the castle of Castile and the lion of León, and the program carves that inlaid edge into its own board.

Fanorona's board is two alquerque boards side by side. In 1886 W. Montgomery found it played in Madagascar "everywhere, in-doors and out of doors, in the town and in the country, and by all classes", its lines scratched on rocks, on market sheds and on the paving outside the palace. The royal histories tell of Prince Andriantompokoindrindra, who would not leave his game to help his father in battle, and lost the kingdom: "Three against five, and toss away the kingdom," goes the proverb. In 2008 five researchers worked out by computer that fanorona, played perfectly, is a draw. Nobody plays perfectly.
