**THE GAME THAT BEAT AN EMPEROR!**

Some time between 474 and 491, the emperor Zeno sits at the board, his men spread round its twenty-four points, and throws three dice: 2, 5 and 6. A poet wrote down exactly where every man stood, and the throw, and what came of it: disaster. Fifteen hundred years later it is the one game position to survive from all of antiquity, and this program's own rules engine plays it out and agrees with the poet. That throw leaves Zeno one legal play, and it leaves eight of his men standing alone, waiting to be hit. "Avoid backgammon," the poem ends, "as the king himself did not escape from its blind chance."

Now it's your turn at the board. Fifteen men each, three dice, and a race round the points and off. Each die moves one man. Two of yours together close a point; a man alone is a blot, and a blot hit goes back to start all over again. Bring everyone into the last six points and bear them off, all fifteen, before your rival does. If that sounds like backgammon, it should: this is its Roman ancestor.

- **Tabula, the colours opposite**: the sides run against each other, as in backgammon, after Becq de Fouquières and Austin, the two who worked out Zeno's game
- **Tabula, the colours the same way**: R. C. Bell's game, both sides running the same way round, so every point is fought over from the first throw
- **Ludus duodecim scriptorum**: the older Roman game of twelve lines, on three rows of twelve, as Cicero and Ovid knew it

Play the computer at three strengths, from either side, or a friend at the same keyboard. Can you do better than the emperor?

## Controls

- **Enter** or **Space**: throw; then arrows to a lit man, **Enter** to pick it up, a lit point to put it down, one die at a time; **Esc**: let go, or take back the last step
- **Mouse**: click
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

Rome left its board games everywhere: boards by the hundred, many cut in stone, dice and dice towers, and a tavern wall at Pompeii where two players quarrel over a throw ("I'm out!" "Not three, it's two!") and the landlord tells them to take it outside. A board found at Timgad spells out the Roman idea of the good life: to hunt, to bathe, to play, to laugh, that is living. The emperor Claudius loved dice so much he had a board fitted to his carriage. But no Roman wrote the rules down, and every set of them is a reconstruction. Agathias' epigram on Zeno's lost game is the one place they can be tested, and the rules hold. From Rome the game went on to Byzantium as *tavli* and to medieval Europe as the game of tables, the backgammon of today.

The ornament here follows the compass rosettes of a board found at Aphrodisias, in Turkey.
