Somewhere nearby is Colossal Cave, where others have found fortunes in treasure and gold, though it is rumored that some who enter are never seen again. You start at the end of a road, before a small brick building. Take what you need, find the way in, and bring the treasures back. The cave is full of passages, mazes and puzzles, a pirate and some unfriendly dwarves, and words of magic scrawled on its walls.

This is the game that started the genre, in the 430-point version 2.5 that its authors finished in 1995.

## How to play

Type what you want to do, in one or two words, and press Enter:

- **look**, **inventory** (or **i**), **score**
- **north**, **south**, **east**, **west**, **up**, **down** (or **n**, **s**, **e**, **w**, **u**, **d**), and **in**, **out**, **building**
- **take lamp**, **light lamp**, **unlock grate**, **drop bird**
- **help** and **info** explain more; the game offers hints when you seem stuck, at a cost in points
- **save** writes your game to a file you name, and quits; to continue, start a new game and say **resume**
- **quit** to stop

The up and down arrows recall earlier commands. Saved games are kept in `~/.local/share/colossal-cave` (on Windows, `%APPDATA%\colossal-cave`).

## History

Will Crowther wrote Adventure in 1976 in FORTRAN on a PDP-10 at Bolt, Beranek and Newman, modelled on the Colossal Cave complex in Kentucky. Don Woods expanded it at the Stanford AI Lab in 1977 into the 350-point game that spread across the world's computers. The two kept working on it until 1995 and Adventure 2.5, with more cave and 430 points. Eric S. Raymond ported that version to modern C as Open Adventure; it plays the same game, with a command line that remembers.
