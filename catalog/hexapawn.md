**BEAT IT NOW. TOMORROW IT WILL HAVE LEARNED.**

Three pawns each on a board of nine squares. Pawns step straight ahead onto an empty square, or take an enemy pawn diagonally ahead. Reach the far side, take every enemy pawn, or leave the other side with no move, and you win. You can learn the whole game in a minute.

So can your opponent, and that is the point. At the start the computer knows nothing but the rules, and you will beat it again and again. But every game it loses, it strikes out the move that lost, for good: "It will not make that last move again." When every move from a position has been struck out, it resigns rather than walk into the same trap.

Game by game the holes close. Your favourite trick stops working. Then the next one. Play long enough and you are facing a machine that cannot be beaten, and you taught it everything it knows.

- **Its memory on screen:** positions met, moves struck out, the run of recent results, and for the board in front of you, which moves it still trusts and which it has crossed out
- **It remembers between sessions,** so the lesson carries on tomorrow; **R** wipes its memory and it knows only the rules again
- **Mouse, arrows or the number pad**

## Controls

- **Arrows** and **Enter**, or the mouse: pick a pawn, then its square
- **1** to **9**: a square, laid out like the number pad
- **R**: wipe the computer's memory; **Enter**: play again
- **M**: sound on or off; **Esc**: quit

## History

In March 1962 Martin Gardner showed the readers of *Scientific American* how to build a machine that learns, out of matchboxes. He invented Hexapawn for it, a game small enough that the machine needed only 24 boxes, after Donald Michie's matchbox engine for noughts and crosses. Each box held the moves for one position; when the machine lost, the losing move was taken out of its box. With perfect play the player who moves first always loses, and the machine finds that out the only way it can: from you. R. A. Kaapke turned it into a program, Jeff Dalton and Steve North reworked it, and David H. Ahl printed it in *BASIC Computer Games* (1978). Here its memory is kept, so the machine you trained is still there next time.
