**THREE IN A ROW TAKES A MAN! BUILD THE MILL AND GRIND THEM DOWN.**

Nine men each, twenty-four points, and a board of three squares joined at their middles. Place your men one at a time, then slide them along the lines. Three of yours in a row is a mill, and a mill takes one of your rival's men off the board. Open the mill, close it again next turn, and it takes another! Every move builds a mill, blocks one, or sets up the next. Bring your rival down to two men, or leave them no move at all, and you've won.

And when you're down to your last three? In today's game your men fly to any empty point on the board, and a beaten side can still bite back.

- **Nine Men's Morris, today's rules**: the tournament game, with flying for the last three
- **Nine Men's Morris after Bell**: the older game, no flying, no mercy
- **Morabaraba**: South Africa's game of twelve cows each, on the board with diagonals at the corners, where a mill shoots a cow
- **Six Men's Morris**: two squares and six men each
- **Three Men's Morris**: three men each on a little square, where a mill wins outright
- **Three Men's Morris, placing only**: four counters each and no moving, noughts and crosses in pieces
- **The computer at three strengths**, from either side, or a friend at the same keyboard

## Controls

- **Arrows**: move the cursor; **Enter** or **Space**: enter a man, or pick one up and put it down on a lit point; **Esc**: let go, or take back a mill before it bites
- **O**: the carved ornament on or off
- **Mouse**: click a point
- After a mill, the men you may take are lit red: choose one
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

The oldest dated boards are Roman: one on a tile from Enserune in southern France, from about 100 BC to AD 40, and one cut into a tile of the Thirtieth Legion at Xanten before it was fired. Ovid wrote of the little board where the winner makes "his line up together". In the Gokstad ship, buried in Norway about 900, lay an oak board cut for Nine Men's Morris on one side and, on the other, perhaps for hnefatafl. Alfonso X of Castile had the first written rules made in 1283, and R. C. Bell found its boards cut into the cloister seats of Norwich, Canterbury, Gloucester and Salisbury cathedrals and Westminster Abbey. In England shepherds cut it into the turf, and when Titania mourns the ruined summer in A Midsummer Night's Dream, "The nine men's morris is filled up with mud".

Its names come from its pieces: merels from the Latin for a little man, the German Mühle, the mill, from the Roman foot soldier. In southern Africa the board with diagonals, Morabaraba, is played to this day, and in 1996 Ralph Gasser showed by computer that Nine Men's Morris, played perfectly, is a draw. The board here is carved after a sixteenth-century games box from Granada and the knots of the Gokstad board.
