**MACUILXOCHITL! CAST THE BEANS, AND STAKE EVERYTHING!**

A mat on the ground, a great cross painted across it, and a crowd so thick they're climbing over each other to watch. Five black beans, each pierced with a single white hole. You call out the name of the god of the beans, Macuilxochitl, Five Roses, clap your hands, and throw. One hole, one step. Four holes, four. All five holes staring up at you? **Ten!**

Six stones each, red and blue, brought in at the middle and sent once round the whole cross and off. The way your first stone takes is the way every stone you own must go. A compartment holds only one stone, so nobody is ever knocked off: this is a pure race, and a betting race at that. Land on a pinched compartment, where black triangles squeeze the arm, and pay your rival two forfeits. Reach a rounded end and throw again. Get stuck with no move and pay into the pool. The first with all six stones off takes the pool!

The Aztecs played patolli for stakes, and played it hard: when there was nothing else left to stake, they staked themselves. A gambler who lost himself and couldn't buy himself back within the time agreed was the winner's slave for ever. Put your purse on the mat. How far will you go?

- **Five beans, one god**: every cast calls on Macuilxochitl, as the players did
- **The friar's equipment**: the mat, the cross, the twelve stones and the beans as Diego Durán described them around 1579
- **R. C. Bell's rules**: the game as a games historian rebuilt it in 1969, pinched squares, rounded ends and all
- **A purse and a pool**: every forfeit counts, and the winner takes it all

Play the computer at three strengths, as Red or Blue, or a friend at the same keyboard.

## Controls

- **Enter** or **Space**: cast the beans; then arrows to a lit compartment, **Enter** to send a stone there; **B**: bear a stone off
- **Mouse**: click
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

Diego Durán, a Dominican friar in Mexico, wrote the fullest account of patolli around 1579: the mat with its great saltire ruled in melted rubber, the red and blue stones, the beans with their white holes, the gamblers who carried their mats rolled under the arm and their beans tied in a cloth, spoke to them "a thousand words of love", and offered them incense and food before they played. The authorities saw idolatry in it: they tore up the mats and burned the beans, and nobody wrote down how the game was played. In 1969 the games historian R. C. Bell put forward rules of his own on Durán's equipment, and those are the rules here.

The ornament follows the patolli scene of the Codex Magliabechiano: the weave of the mat, and, on a big terminal, its five discs above it.
