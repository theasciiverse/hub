**ONE FOX. THIRTEEN GEESE. SOMEBODY GETS EATEN!**

The fox is in the farmyard. He is quick, he is hungry, and he can jump: over a goose and onto the empty point beyond, and that goose is gone. Over another, and another, all in one move, if the gaggle gets careless. The geese can't take anything at all. They can only waddle, one step at a time, closer and closer, until the fox has nowhere left to run.

Play the fox and it's a hunt: find the gap, wait for the slip, pick the gaggle apart. Thin them down to three and you've won, because three geese can never trap a fox. Play the geese and it's a siege: hold the line, never leave a goose with an empty point behind it, and squeeze. R. C. Bell wrote that if the geese are played right, the fox must lose. Are you the gaggle to prove him right, or the fox to prove him wrong?

- **Thirteen geese**: the older form, where geese step any way along a line
- **Seventeen geese**: four more geese in the line, but none may ever step back. Wedge them in and the fox wins anyway!
- **Chains of jumps**: the fox may turn between jumps and stop when he likes; point at a landing and every goose the jump would eat turns red
- **The computer at three strengths**, as fox or geese, or a friend at the same keyboard

## Controls

- **Arrows**: move the cursor
- **Enter**: pick up a piece, then put it down on a lit point; **Esc**: drop it
- **Mouse**: click a piece, then a point
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

Hunt games like this are old in the north. R. C. Bell gathered the clues: a fox game, hala-tafl, named in the Icelandic Grettis saga, and, in the accounts of the household of Edward IV of England, two foxes and twenty-six hounds of silver over-gilt bought for two sets of the game. Across Europe it went by many names: Renard et les poules in France, Fuchs und Gänse in Germany, Rävspel in Sweden, Refskák in Iceland. Its cross of thirty-three points was so familiar that when a new patience for one player reached England from the Continent in the eighteenth century, Solitaire, it was played on a fox and geese board. In the 1840s J. M. Kronheim & Co. of London sold the fox and his geese in a box, and in 1879 the Boy's Own Paper gave its readers a board.

The board here is decorated after a games box probably made in Granada in the sixteenth century, now in the Victoria and Albert Museum, whose slide carries fox and geese beside merelles and chess, with ivory stars and rosettes inlaid in the corners of the cross.
