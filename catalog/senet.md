**PASS THROUGH THE NETHERWORLD, AND MIND THE HOUSE OF WATER!**

Thirty houses in three rows of ten. Four throwing sticks. Your pieces against theirs, both racing the same way down the S-shaped track, so every move is a shove: pass them, block them, swap places with a lonely enemy and send him back where you came from. Guard your pieces in pairs. Line up three and nobody gets by.

Then the last houses, the ones the old boards marked with signs. Every piece must stop on the House of Beauty. One house further lies the House of Water, and fall in there and you are swept back to the House of Rebirth! Beyond it the Three Truths, Re-Atoum and Horus, the last house, each wanting just the right throw to let you off. The gods are not in a hurry.

For three thousand years Egypt played this game. Hatshepsut's name is on one board, Amenhotep III's on another, a board went into Tutankhamun's tomb, and Queen Nefertari sits at the board on the wall of hers. In time the game took on a deeper meaning: the dead played it against an invisible opponent, and the race became the soul's passage through the netherworld. Its very name, *senet*, means passing.

Nobody wrote the rules down. What survives is the boards, the sticks, and tomb paintings of people having a wonderful time. So choose your reconstruction:

- **Kendall, five pieces**: the rules on most Senet sets sold today
- **Kendall, seven pieces**: Timothy Kendall's 1978 original, where a piece fallen into the water freezes your whole side until you fish it out
- **R. C. Bell 1960**: ten men a side, a long die, and capture by landing: whoever bears off the most men wins

Play the computer at three strengths, or a friend at the same keyboard.

## Controls

- **Space** or **Enter**: throw
- **Arrows** or **Tab**: choose a move; **Enter**: make it
- **R**: rescue a piece from the water (Kendall, seven pieces)
- **Mouse**: click to throw, then click a lit piece
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and what is known; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same throws every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

Boards that could be senet lie in First Dynasty burials of about 3100 BC, and the first certain picture of the game is in the tomb of the official Hesy, about 2620 BC. Many boards were boxes with a drawer for the pieces and sticks, often with a second game on the underside: Twenty Squares, the later Royal Game of Ur. The game fell out of use in the Roman period, and its rules went with it. Gustave Jéquier proposed rules from the boards and the pictures; then came R. C. Bell's, and in 1978 Timothy Kendall's, printed in a booklet sold with his own senet set under the title *Passing Through the Netherworld*. The makers of modern sets took them up, and most of them play Kendall's game of five.

The ornament round the board here follows the faience box of Amenhotep III in the Brooklyn Museum: the chequer of its drawer and its panels of ankh and djed.
