**SOW THE SEEDS. COUNT THEM TWICE. TAKE THE HARVEST!**

Two rows of pits, a heap of seeds, and one move: lift every seed from a pit and sow them, one by one, into the pits that follow. Where the last one lands decides everything. Make a two or a three on your rival's side in Oware and they're yours. Drop it into your own store in Kalah and go again! Lose count, and you've just fed your opponent.

Mancala isn't one game but hundreds, played from West Africa to the Philippines, from Cairo's coffee houses to the Kazakh steppe. They all sow. After that they argue about nearly everything, and here are nine of the arguments, each played by its own rules:

- **Oware (Abapa)**: Ghana's "proper version", the tournament game; take twos and threes on the other side, but never the whole row
- **Wari**: R. C. Bell's game from Ghana, where taking everything is simply not allowed
- **Mankal'ah, the game of the ignorant** and **the game of the wise**: played for coffee in the Cairo of the 1830s, with 72 pebbles set out by hand. Don't like how they were set out? Turn the board round, and give up the first move!
- **Kalah**: the American game most people now call Mancala
- **Congkak**: the game of the Malay rajas' courts, on a boat-shaped board, where the sowing runs on and on; empty out and you're left with ruined wells, then utterly destroyed
- **Pallanguli**: the Tamil game, where four seeds in a hole make a cow for its owner
- **Toguz Kumalak**: nine holes of nine stones from the Kazakh steppe, where three stones in an enemy hole make it yours for good
- **Bohnenspiel**: the bean game a Shah of Persia gave to Catherine the Great

Some games run over several boards: Kalah to 40 points, Lane's Cairo games to 60, Pallanguli round after round until someone is cleaned out. Play the computer at three strengths, from either side, or a friend at the same keyboard.

## Controls

- **Left, Right**: choose a pit; **Enter**: sow it
- **Mouse**: click a pit
- **Any key or click** while seeds are moving: finish the move
- **Lane's games**: **Up** and **Down** drop a pebble in or take one out, **Tab** jumps to the other row, **R** scatters the rest, **Enter** when your hand is empty; then **T** turns the board round, **Enter** keeps it
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides, going through the games in turn
- `--seed N`: the same game every time

## History

Thomas Hyde of Oxford gave Europe its first account of the game in 1694, and its name: manqala, so called "because they move the pebbles from place to place". The likeliest early boards were cut in limestone at Matara in Eritrea, a town of the kingdom of Aksum, in the 6th to 8th centuries AD. In 1623 Richard Jobson, trading on the Gambia river, saw men with "certaine great holes cut" in a piece of wood and "some thirtie pibble stones", who "after a manner of counting, they take one from the other, until one is possessed of all". E. W. Lane watched it played for coffee in the coffee shops of Cairo in the 1830s and wrote down its first full rules.

It crossed the ocean with enslaved Africans: in Suriname and the Caribbean it was played in the house of mourning to amuse the dead, and never after dark, when the spirits of the dead would join in. In 1908 Fritz Jahn found the bean game at Kardis in the Baltic provinces, copied from the one a Shah of Persia had given the Empress Catherine II more than a hundred years before. In 1955 W. J. Champion of Mystic, Connecticut, was granted his patent for Kalah, and in 2020 UNESCO inscribed the mancala games of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Türkiye on its list of the intangible heritage of humanity.
