**OPEN IT LATE. NOT TOO LATE. SPLAT!**

You're at the open door. Thousands of feet below is a world picked at random: Mars, Venus, the Moon, Jupiter, Neptune... or the Sun. You're told the height, the pull of gravity and your terminal speed, and every one of them could be five per cent off. Set the timer that pops your chute. Then jump.

Down you go through the column of sky, with eight reports on the way. Open too high and it's a dull jump, and a little cowardly. Open too low and, well: *SPLAT. A small crater marks the spot; may it rest in peace.*

The real game is your own nerve. Every good jump is measured against all the ones before it. Later than every one of them? *Superb nerve!* Higher than nearly all? *Were you nervous?*

- **Ten worlds** with their real gravity, from the gentle Moon to the Sun's 899 feet per second, every second
- **Watch the fall** down a column of sky, the ground coming up to meet you
- **Your nerve, ranked:** each good jump against your earlier ones, by how close to the ground you opened

## Controls

- **0** to **9** and **.**: the free-fall timer in seconds; **↑ ↓**: ±0.5
- **Enter**: jump; **Tab**: a different jump
- **M**: sound on or off; **Esc**: quit

## History

John Yegge wrote *Splat* at Oak Ridge Associated Universities in Tennessee: a skydiving game with a physics lesson folded inside, gravity and terminal velocity on any world out to Neptune. David H. Ahl printed it in *BASIC Computer Games* (1978). It is rebuilt here with today's figures for every world's gravity, and the same small crater for the overconfident.
