**TEN YEARS ON THE THRONE. WILL THEY SING YOUR NAME, OR CURSE IT?**

You take the seal of a city of a hundred souls, a thousand acres of land and a granary of grain. Every spring your stewards wait on three orders: buy land or sell it, open the granary to the people, and sow the fields. Each soul eats twenty bushels a year and can work ten acres, and every two acres want a bushel of seed. Get the sums wrong and somebody goes hungry.

Then the gods roll the dice. The fields give five bushels an acre, or one. Rats get into the stores. Newcomers crowd the gates in the good years, and plague can take half the city in a single night. Let too many starve in one year and the city rises: you are dragged from the palace and sent into exile.

Hold on for ten years and the people judge you. A golden age, where they sing your name and swear no ruler of Sumer has done better? Or a reign remembered with dread? Either way, they tell you exactly what held you back.

- **A report every year,** in plain words: the harvest, the rats, the newcomers, the plague
- **Your reign in a chart:** people, grain and land, year by year
- **Live arithmetic:** every order shows what it leaves in store, and warns you before anyone goes hungry
- **The people's verdict,** from a golden age down to exile, with the reason for it: too many went hungry, or the fields never grew with the city

## Controls

- **Tab**, **← →** or a click: move between the orders
- **↑ ↓**, **PgUp PgDn** or the wheel: change a number; typing replaces it
- **F**: feed everyone; **P**: plant all you can
- **Enter**: end the year
- **M**: sound on or off; **Esc**: quit

## History

In 1964 Mabel Addis, an elementary-school teacher, wrote and designed *The Sumerian Game*, in which children ruled the city of Lagash in 3500 BC. Doug Dyment heard it described after a talk at the University of Alberta and made his own at DEC in 1968, for the brand-new FOCAL language: *The Sumer Game*, small enough to run on a minimal PDP-8. Around 1971 David H. Ahl put it into BASIC as *Hamurabi*, and BASIC ran nearly everywhere. Printed in *BASIC Computer Games*, the best-selling computer book of its day with over a million copies sold, it became a rite of passage: Jerry Pournelle remembered that half the people he knew wrote a Hammurabi program in the 1970s. Asciiverse wrote this one anew in 2026, with the old city's hunger and its arithmetic intact.
