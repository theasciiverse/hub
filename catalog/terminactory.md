**THE GROUND IS YOURS, SO DIG ANYWHERE. NO RUSH.**

So says the first telegram, from Hollowmere Mining Co., who have dug these hills for four generations, and the Brannock sisters, all three of them, who will sell you furnaces, belts and a solar panel to run them. Put a drill down anywhere and tell it what to dig. Feed it coal. Belt the copper ore into a furnace, the molten copper into a molder, the ingots into a press and the plates into a trade post, and listen to the money come in. Then make it bigger.

Terminactory is a factory game that never hurries you. Nothing attacks, nothing breaks at random, and the factory keeps earning while the game is closed. A machine that stops tells you why in one word, **FULL**, **NEED** or **EC?**, and the fix is yours to find. Make something new and a new company writes to you: copper wire, and Ashgrove Ironstone says *"Copper wire, is it? Fine hands."* Coke, and Cinderhall Foundry shouts *"Now you're cooking."* A girder, and Pillar & Sons will raise you a floor above the ground. Your very first chip, and Meridian Instruments has been waiting for you, and brought snacks.

- **From copper to computers**: steel from blast furnaces, steam engines, concrete and glass, assemblers from crude to precision, silicon wafers, microprocessors, and at the top, the computer. Then oil
- **Up and down**: floors raised on girders, floors dug beneath you where the ore is richer, and elevators to carry it all between them
- **Fifteen companies**, each with its own voice and its own machines, from Kettering & Vane, two partners who met over a boiler that would not stop whistling and have been discussing it since (*"Mr Kettering says your coil is crude. Mr Vane says it's charming."*), to the alarmingly friendly Meridian
- **Research that rewards variety**: selling the same thing pays less research each time, so a new product is always worth making
- **Tools for big builds**: belt tunnels, splitters and overflow gates, an area tool, an upgrade planner, and blueprints you can turn, mirror and keep in a library

Press **b** and a browser opens. **termweb** has tabs, bookmarks and a search page, a wiki with a page for every item and machine, and behind it a whole small internet. The Municipal Gazette reports that the Mill Lane resurfacing is postponed until after the frosts, and remembers Moll Peck, whose ad for a mangle ran from Y124 to Y131 and never answered a single reply. On crankshaft.forum, GerdV asks whether anyone else remembers the first furnace, and old_bert_hands has been telling the story of the coal spill of Y127 for nine years, though it was not him, "which is how a thing becomes mine." The company sites have their histories, and the plant intranets are locked: your password is your staff number, unless you are one of the few given a word, and the words are out there in what people say to one another. Read long enough and Plant 7 begins to feel very calm. You can play without ever opening it, or read it all and barely build. Either way is the game.

## Controls

- **Arrows** or **WASD**: move; **Shift+arrows**: lay belts as you go
- **1** to **4**: build menu, then a number picks the part; **Space** or a click: place
- **r**: rotate; **f**: set it up (ore, recipe, money or research); **x** or right-click: remove
- **t**: research; **o**: production; **b**: the browser; **g**: settings
- **c**: area tool; **u**: upgrade; **l**: blueprint library; **m**: mirror; **z**: undo a removal
- **<** **>**: floors; **v**: power range; **n**: next telegram; **i**: telegram log
- **p**: pause; **[** **]**: speed; **Ctrl+S**: save; **?**: every key; **Ctrl+C**: save and quit

## Options

- `--demo`: start beside a small factory that already works
- `--new`: start a fresh save; `--saves`: list your saves; `terminactory 2`: open save 2

## History

snipedbywifi started Terminactory on 10 November 2026 and showed it to the world four days and some two hundred commits later: an idle factory meant to sit in a terminal pane beside your work, switching to a compact view when the pane is small and earning while you look away. It grew fast, from copper and coal to steam, floors and assemblers, then silicon and oil, but the part that grew most was the browser. What began on the third day as a recipe book became a town, its newspaper, its companies, five forums and the plants' own message boards, written page by page across forty years of an invented calendar.
