**FOURTH AND ONE. GRANITE FALLS IS STACKING THE LINE. WHAT'S THE CALL?**

Coin toss. Kickoff. Four fifteen-minute quarters, your Northfield against Granite Falls, and every single down is yours to call.

Pound it up the middle. Run the sweep. Hit the short pass, gamble on the long bomb, or slip a screen under the blitz. Punt it away, or line up the field goal with its distance on the board. On defence, stack the line, play it balanced, drop back in coverage or send the blitz, and hope you guessed their play before they ran it.

The other coach is reading you too. He kicks the field goal inside your 38, goes for it on fourth and one, and throws on third and long. Out-think him for sixty minutes. Sacks, fumbles, interceptions, safeties and the breakaway run you never saw coming are all waiting on the hundred-yard field.

- **Seven plays and four defences,** and every one a counter to another
- **The ball in flight** down the field, the line to gain marked, with play-by-play
- **The field goal's distance** shown before you try it
- **Your record:** wins, losses, ties and the biggest margin

## Controls

- **1** to **7**, **↑ ↓** and **Enter**, or a click: call the play
- **Enter**: the second half, and a new game at the end
- **N**: a new game; **Tab**: the rules; **M**: sound on or off; **Esc**: quit

## History

In 1965 Dartmouth beat Princeton in an upset, and on the Sunday after, John G. Kemeny, one of the two men who created BASIC, sat down and wrote a football game: *FTBALL*, where you call numbered plays and the bounce of the ball decides the rest. David H. Ahl printed it in *BASIC Computer Games* (1978), beside other football games. This one is built fresh from the idea: you call the plays, and the computer calls its own.
