**FOUR MEN EACH. ONE EMPTY POINT. NOWHERE TO HIDE!**

An eight-pointed star drawn round a centre, and the Māori who played it saw an octopus: the centre is the pūtahi, the arms are its tentacles, the kewai. You have four men, your rival has four, and there is always exactly one empty point. Move a man into it, if a line joins them. Nothing is ever taken, nothing is ever crowned. The side that cannot move is piro, out, and has lost.

It sounds simple. It's a knife-edge! Step into the pūtahi at the wrong moment and you hand your rival the one move that blocks you dead. Every position can be counted, and the program counts them all before the first move: on hard, the computer never makes a mistake. Can you hold it to a draw?

- **The pūtahi beside an enemy**: today's rule; a man may step into the centre only from beside an enemy man, and two perfect players draw
- **Mū tōrere after Best**: the rule exactly as Tuta Nihoniho gave it, where it guards only the opening move and the side that moves second can win at once; play it and see how
- **The computer at three strengths**, from either side, or a friend at the same keyboard

## Controls

- **Arrows**: move the cursor; **Enter** or **Space**: move the man under it; the men that may move are lit
- **Mouse**: click a man
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time

## History

Mū tōrere belongs to the East Coast of New Zealand's North Island and the people of Ngāti Porou. Its star was drawn in charcoal on a hewn plank, or on the inner bark of the tōtara, marked while it was green and kept flat with sticks as it dried, or simply scratched in the earth with a pointed stick. Mohi Turei of Ngāti Porou told Elsdon Best in 1912 that mū tōrere was the game's old name, and Tuta Nihoniho explained it to him, with a whole game move by move; Best published it in Games and Pastimes of the Maori in 1925. How old it is, nobody knows: some of Best's informants held it was played before the Europeans came, others that it arrived with the early whalers. Te Ara, the encyclopedia of New Zealand, tells that tōrere was a popular game on the East Coast from the very early years of European contact.
