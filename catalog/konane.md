**EVERY MOVE IS A JUMP. THE LAST ONE TO JUMP WINS!**

Every hollow of the papamū starts full, black and white in turn, without a single gap. Two stones are lifted out, and from then on every move is a jump: leap one of your rival's stones into the empty hollow beyond, and it's yours. Keep going along the same line, if the gaps line up, and take two, three, four in one sweep. The board empties, lanes open and slam shut, and the first player who cannot jump has lost.

Every jump you make opens a hollow your rival might use. Every stone you take might have been the one blocking their last escape. Count the moves left, yours and theirs, and leave them nothing! When James King saw it in 1779, he thought it "much more intricate" than draughts.

- **Kōnane after Ernst**: the classic 8 by 8, with jumps that run on in a straight line
- **Modern Kōnane**: one jump a move, and every one of them counts
- **Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau**: the park's own rules, on the 9 by 11 board of its old stone papamū
- **King's papamū**: the great board of 238 squares, fourteen by seventeen, that James King counted in 1779
- **The computer at three strengths**, from either side, or a friend at the same keyboard

## Controls

- **Arrows**: move the cursor; **Enter** or **Space**: lift a stone at the start, or pick one up and land it on a lit hollow; **Esc**: let go
- **Mouse**: click
- The stones a jump would take turn red when the cursor is on its landing
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

Kōnane was played all across the Hawaiian islands with beach-worn pebbles, coral for white and lava for black, on boards of hollows cut in stone or marked in wood, and boards cut into the lava survive at many places on the islands. In 1779 James King, an officer on Captain Cook's third voyage, saw a board about two feet long with 238 squares, fourteen in a row, and wrote that the game was "very much like our draughts" but "much more intricate". The old boards came in every size, from 8 to 20 hollows a row. The game played today comes down through Kenneth Emory, Peter Buck and Michael Ernst, with its Hawaiian words: piko, the centre; kahola, a jump over several stones. And the leaflet of Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau tells that King Kamehameha "was an excellent player, sometimes beating his opponent in one move", and that "his chiefs and queens were no match for him".
