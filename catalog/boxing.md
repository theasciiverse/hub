**THREE ROUNDS. ONE WEAK SPOT. FIND HIS BEFORE HE FINDS YOURS!**

The bell rings. Across the ring stands Dario Fenn, or Tomas Vela, or Jules Okoro, Kasimir Lund or Ren Ishida, and every one of them is hiding two secrets: a best punch that lands with extra force, and a weak spot that folds when you hit it. You have both too. Choose them before the bout, and guard them well.

Then it's eight exchanges a round. Jab, cross, hook, uppercut, or cover up and get your wind back. Land on his weak spot and it all comes out; take his best punch on the chin and now you know what it is. And he is learning yours just as fast. Go after what you've found, keep your guard up, and don't punch yourself out in the first round.

Knockdowns bring the count. Three in a round and the referee waves it off. Go the distance and three judges decide it on their cards, and they don't always agree.

- **Five opponents,** each with his own secret best punch and weak spot
- **What you've learned about him,** on screen as you learn it
- **Three judges** scoring every round, a point off for every knockdown, split decisions and draws
- **A sporting finish:** the two boxers touch gloves and embrace in the centre of the ring, whoever wins
- **Your record:** wins, early wins, losses and draws

## Controls

- **1** to **4**: your best punch, then your weak spot
- **1** to **5**, **↑ ↓** and **Enter**, or a click: each exchange
- **Enter**: the next round; **N**: a new bout; **Tab**: the rules; **M**: sound on or off; **Esc**: quit

## History

A three-round Olympic boxing match was printed in David H. Ahl's *101 BASIC Computer Games* (1973), and again in *BASIC Computer Games* (1978): two boxers, each with a best punch and a weak spot, and a player choosing the punches one by one. Its idea of hidden strengths, found out blow by blow, is the heart of this one too, now with a referee, a count and three judges.
