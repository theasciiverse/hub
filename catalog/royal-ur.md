**FORTY-FIVE CENTURIES OLD, AND STILL A KNIFE FIGHT ON TWENTY SQUARES!**

Twenty squares of inlaid shell, five of them blazing with rosettes. Seven pieces a side. Throw the lots and run: down your own safe row, out onto the long middle row where both armies meet, and home. Land on an enemy there and send it all the way back to the start! Land on a rosette and throw again! And the rosette in the very middle of the shared row is the square everybody wants: hold it, and nobody can touch you.

This game was played in the city of Ur around 2600 BC, and across the Near East for more than two thousand years after. Leonard Woolley dug its boards out of the Royal Cemetery in the 1920s, and for decades nobody knew how to play. Then a clay tablet, written in Babylon in 177 or 176 BC, catalogued for seventy years as bird omens, turned out to hold the oldest known rules of any board game on Earth. Irving Finkel of the British Museum read them, and the game came back to life.

So which game will you play? Four reconstructions are here:

- **Finkel 2017**: the rules most people play today, as Finkel played them against Tom Scott for the British Museum
- **British Museum 1990s**: five pieces and no safe square anywhere, a meaner race
- **R. C. Bell 1960**: a betting game! Pieces enter only on a five, every rosette makes your opponent pay into the pool, and your purse carries from game to game
- **The Babylonian tablet**: five birds, the Swallow, the Storm-bird, the Raven, the Rooster and the Eagle, each brought on by its own throw; a sheep's knucklebone, and an ox's you can **dare** for a bigger throw or nothing at all; rosettes that pay out to whoever lands and charge whoever passes

Lose your whole purse and you will know why Babylon kept its accounts in clay. Play the computer at three strengths, or a friend at the same keyboard.

## Controls

- **Space** or **Enter**: throw
- **Arrows** or **Tab**: choose a piece; **Enter**: move it
- **D**: dare the ox bone (tablet rules)
- **Mouse**: click to throw, then click a lit piece
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same throws every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

The board of twenty squares turns up from Iraq and Iran to Egypt and Crete, over a hundred of them, and was played for more than two thousand years. Woolley's excavations at Ur, 1922 to 1934, gave it its modern name; one of his boards, inlaid with shell, is in the British Museum. The tablet came to the museum around 1880, and T. G. Pinches catalogued it in 1886 as bird omens, for it names a swallow and a raven. Only in 1960 did Landsberger show it was about a game, and Irving Finkel worked out the game it describes in 2007. The Jews of Kochi in India were still playing the game, as *asha*, in the 1950s.

The board's ornament here follows the one in the British Museum: its eight-petalled rosettes, red and blue in turn, and the row of shell lozenges round its edge.
