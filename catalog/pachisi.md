**TWENTY-FIVE! THE THROW THAT GAVE THE GAME ITS NAME!**

Unroll the cloth: a great cross of four arms, red, black, yellow and green. Two partnerships, partners sitting opposite. Six cowrie shells rattle in your hand and fall: count the mouths. The best fall of all is worth 25, *pachis*, and you throw again!

Every man must come out of the middle, run right round the outside of the whole cross against the sun, and climb back up his own arm home: eighty-four steps. Land on a lone enemy and he goes all the way back to the middle, and you throw again! Only on the castles, the crossed squares, is anyone safe. The partnership with all eight men home first wins.

At the emperor Akbar's court, around 1590, as many as two hundred players would sit down to this game, and nobody went home before sixteen games were done, which could take three months. Anyone who lost patience and got restless had to drink a cup of wine. Can you keep your nerve?

- **Pachisi after Tylor**: six cowries and the rules as Rajendralala Mitra of Calcutta gave them in 1879, castles and all, and three big throws in a row rot away to nothing
- **Pachisi after Falkener**: the rules of 1892 that R. C. Bell reprinted, where a crowd of men on an open square is at its own peril: one enemy landing there takes them all!
- **Chaupar after Abu'l-Fazl**: the game of Akbar's court, with three long dice, men already out on the board, no castles, and doublets that march a pair forward together

Play the computer at three strengths, from either partnership, or a friend at the same keyboard; each side plays both colours of its partnership.

## Controls

- **Enter** or **Space**: throw; then arrows to a lit man, **Enter** to pick it up, a lit square to put it down; men sharing a square can march on together; **Esc**: let go; **P**: leave the rest of the throws unused
- **Mouse**: click
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

The cross-shaped game turns up in Indian poems and paintings of the 15th and 16th centuries, and the first account of its rules is Abu'l-Fazl's, in the *Ain-i Akbari*, the great record of Akbar's court: "From times of old, the people of Hindústán have been fond of this game." Played with dice it was chaupar; with cowries, pachisi. The board was a cloth, often embroidered, and keen players carried one rolled up in their turbans. Every player taught it a little differently ("one native gives one description of the game, and another another," wrote Edward Falkener in 1892), and in the 1880s the English made Ludo out of it.

On a big terminal, the flowers in the corners beside the cross come from a painting of Maharaja Sovan Singh playing pachisi, made at Udaipur around 1868.
