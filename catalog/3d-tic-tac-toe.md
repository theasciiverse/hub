**TIC-TAC-TOE GREW UP. NOW IT HAS 76 WAYS TO BEAT YOU.**

Forget the little board where every game ends in a draw. This is a cube: four levels, each four by four, sixty-four cells in all. Place your X, the computer places its O, and the first to get four in a line wins.

A line can run along a level, straight down through all four levels, diagonally across a level or down a slice, or from corner to corner right through the heart of the cube. Seventy-six lines, and you have to watch every one of them at once. The computer will. On hard it sets traps of its own: two lines of three in a single move, and you can only block one.

So set your own. Build quietly on level two while it's busy on level four, then spring the double threat. When the winning line lights up across all four levels, you'll know you saw the cube in three dimensions.

- **All four levels side by side,** with the winning line lit up across them
- **Three strengths:** easy forgets to block now and then, hard plots forks against you
- **Turns to start alternate,** since the first move is worth a lot
- **Your record** of wins, losses and draws at every strength

## Controls

- **← → ↑ ↓**: move on a level; **PgUp** and **PgDn** (or **[** and **]**): change level; **1** to **4**: jump to a level
- **Enter**, **Space** or a click: place your X
- **L**: the computer's strength for the next game; **N**: a new game; **Tab**: the rules
- **M**: sound on or off; **Esc**: quit

## History

Parker Brothers sold four-by-four-by-four tic-tac-toe as a board game called Qubic from 1964. A program to play it turned up, author unknown, on a General Electric time-sharing system in 1968, and David H. Ahl printed it as *Qubic* in *101 BASIC Computer Games* (1973) and as *3-D Tic-Tac-Toe* in *BASIC Computer Games* (1978). In 1980 Oren Patashnik proved, with a computer's help, that the first player can always win with perfect play. Nobody plays perfectly, though, which is why it is still such fun.
