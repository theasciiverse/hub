**"Working 14 hours a day in the core of some dusty, smelly mine is not your idea of the perfect lifestyle."**

Barre-An is a dust ball in space, and its mines are not as generous as they used to be. All you ask for is a break. So when two dusty alien miners slap the dust off their pants and sit down at the western table of the tavern, you buy a mug of glunk lite, take the next table and lean in. The foreman has dug up an ancient artifact. Long before the colony, the planet's native race made sacrifices to a god, and their god was a crystal, worth a few million goolaas, waiting on a ritual planet beyond the Sagan system. They even say the coordinates.

Your ship is in the tavern's parking lot, its lab wrecked by one too many rough landings, but it still flies. Out past the coordinates a huge red star burns over a jungle planet, and in the jungle stands a pyramid of metal. Inside are a hall of mirrors where the real passages hide among their reflections, rooms of bones from old sacrifices, and a chamber with a glass floor over a rushing stream of acid, where the crystal, when it finally falls, has to be caught before it reaches the glass.

That crystal is your fortune. It might also be a whole people's last chance, because the red star is not well. Somewhere in this system a vast planetship waits in orbit, its people asleep in endless rows of cryogenic tubes, a cracked crystal in its reactor and defense robots in its tunnels. Keep yourself fed, watered and rested (the panel at the top of the screen tells you when you are not) and there are 1000 points to win before the star becomes a supernova.

## How to play

Type what you want to do and press Enter, in plain English; the game knows over 1000 words. Several commands go on one line, joined by periods or THEN: **open the hatch then enter the ship**. To talk to someone, name them first: **aliens, can you tell me about the crystal**.

- **north**, **south**, **east**, **west** and the diagonals (or **n**, **se** and so on), **up**, **down**; aboard your ship, **fore**, **aft**, **port** and **starboard**
- The arrows, **Home**, **End**, **PgUp** and **PgDn** walk the eight directions; **Insert** goes down and **Delete** up
- **examine** (or **ex**), **look** (or **l**), **inventory** (or **i**), **get**, **drop**, **eat**, **drink**, **wear**, **buy**, **give**, **throw**, **put**, **turn** a dial to a number, **type** on a keyboard
- **clue** for a hint, at a cost in points; **score**; **wait** (or **z**); **repeat** (or **r**)
- **F1** save, **F2** restore, **F3** room descriptions, **F4** look, **F5** and **F6** get and drop everything, **F7** score, **F8** inventory, **F9** wait, **F10** repeat
- **save** and **restore** by name, **restart**, **quit**
- **Shift+PgUp** and **Shift+PgDn** page back through the story

Saved games live in `~/.local/state/supernova`.

## History

In 1985 Scott Miller, still writing about video games for The Dallas Morning News, started his second text adventure on the engine of his first, Beyond the Titanic. Its own notes say it was developed from January to June 1985 on an IBM PC with two disk drives and a colour monitor ("a good one!"), with thanks to Borland, whose Turbo Pascal it was written in. Terry Nagy shaped the story with him.

It came out in 1987 as shareware: if you enjoyed it, the author asked for ten dollars by cheque, and you could call him, or Terry Nagy, for help. What Miller learned from these two games led him to a new idea, giving away the first episode of a game and selling the rest, and that same year he founded Apogee on it. Supernova joined the Apogee catalog, and for a while you could even buy its source code, for $100.

In March 1998 Apogee gave Supernova away as freeware, and on March 20, 2009 released its source as free software. It still only ran in an emulator until November 2014, when Jason Self, fresh from reviving Beyond the Titanic, went back for it. About 20 hours of work later ("or more? I lost track", he wrote), it built and ran on modern systems again.
