**"SACRIFICE YOUR TWO ROOKS, AND NOT ME!"**

So the story goes: a player had staked his beloved, Dilaram, on a game of shatranj, and when this position came up on the board she cried out to him the way to win. The problem is as-Suli's, the greatest master of the game, and it travelled far: copied into Alfonso X's *Book of Games* of 1283, it became the seed of nearly two hundred problems in medieval Europe. This program's own rules engine checks every move of its solution.

This is chess as it was played a thousand years ago, at the court of the caliphs. The shah, the horse and the chariot move as the king, knight and rook do today. But the firzan, the counsellor, only creeps one square on the diagonal, and the fil, the elephant, leaps exactly two, over anything in its way. No queen sweeping the board, no bishop raking it from corner to corner: armies close slowly, every man counts, and games are won and lost in the ending. And there is more than one way to win! Mate the enemy shah. Strip away every last man he has and leave him **bare**. Or shut him in, without a single move, and win by stalemate.

- **Shatranj**: the game of the Baghdad masters, al-'Adli and as-Suli; stalemate the enemy and you win, but a bared shah who can take your last man straight back saves a draw
- **The Medinese victory**: as the people of the Hijaz played it, where whoever bares the other's shah first wins on the spot
- **Chaturanga, the Indian rules**: where the shah left without a move is the winner
- **The elephant in the corner**: another Indian game, the elephant starting in the corner and leaping two squares straight along the ranks and files
- **Al-Biruni's elephant**: "the trunk and the four feet", one more elephant from India

Play the computer at three strengths, from either side, or a friend at the same keyboard.

## Controls

- **Arrows**: move the cursor; **Enter** or **Space**: pick up a man, then put it on a lit square; **Esc**: let go
- **Mouse**: click
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

Chess began in India as *chaturanga*, "of four members": the four arms of an Indian army, chariots, horse, elephants and foot. A Persian tale tells how a king of India sent the game to Khusraw I to test the wisdom of the Persians, and how the sage Wazurgmihr worked it out. The Arabs learned it when they conquered Persia and called it *shatranj*, and in Baghdad, in the 9th and 10th centuries, it found its masters. Al-'Adli wrote on it around 840. As-Suli, the greatest of them all, played at the court of the caliphs, and the caliph ar-Radi once said "As-Suli's skill at chess charms me more than these flowers." Their books of openings, endings and problems survive, over 1,600 problems in the manuscripts. From Islam the game went on to Europe, where in time the counsellor became the queen and the elephant the bishop.

Here the board is a plain cloth ruled into squares, as the old boards were, and the sides are red and black, as the manuscripts drew them.
