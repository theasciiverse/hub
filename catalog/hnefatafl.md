**SAVE THE KING! TWENTY-FOUR RAIDERS STAND BETWEEN HIM AND THE CORNERS.**

The king sits on his throne in the middle of the board, his twelve guards in a diamond round him. On every edge wait the attackers: twenty-four of them, twice as many, and closing in. The king must get out. Reach a corner and he is safe, and his side has won. Box him in on all four sides, and the hall falls.

Every piece slides like a rook, so walls go up and come crashing down in a single move. Trap an enemy between two of your own and it's gone; the corners and the empty throne bite too. The defenders run, feint and sacrifice to open one clear lane; the attackers build a ring and tighten it, man by man. One slip, and the king is through!

The Norse rules are lost, so choose your saga:

- **Copenhagen 11x11**: the tournament game, where a row along the edge can fall all at once as a shield wall, and the king can win by building an exit fort no attacker can break
- **Fetlar 11x11**: the rules of the Shetland island where the World Quickplay Hnefatafl Championships are held; a corner or nothing
- **Brandubh 7x7**: "black raven", the quick Irish game, a king and four against eight
- **Tablut 9x9, after Linnaeus**: eight Swedes and their king against sixteen Muscovites, as the Sámi played it in Lapland in 1732, and the king escapes to any edge
- **Tablut 9x9, after Bell 1960**: the same game as an 1811 mistranslation left it, with a king only four can take. He shouts **Raichi!** when he sees one way out and **Tuichi!** when he sees two. It is not fair, and it is fun
- **The computer at three strengths**, from either side, or a friend at the same keyboard

## Controls

- **Arrows**: move the cursor
- **Enter**: pick up a piece, then put it down on a lit square; **Esc**: drop it
- **Mouse**: click a piece, then a square
- **F1** or **?**: the rules and where they come from; **M**: menu; **Q**: quit
- **O**: the braille ornament on or off

## Options

- `--demo`: the computer plays both sides
- `--seed N`: the same game every time
- `--plain`: start without the ornament, for fonts that draw braille thin

## History

Tafl means "table" in Old Norse, and by the end of the Viking Age the game of the king was hnefatafl, from hnefi, "fist", the name of the king piece. It was played in Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Iceland, Britain, Ireland and Sápmi. Its boards turn up in a Danish grave from before AD 400 and on the oak board of the Gokstad ship burial of about 900, and a little glass piece crowned with white droplets came out of the ground on Lindisfarne in 2019. Earl Rögnvald of Orkney put skill at tafl first among his nine boasts. Then, from the twelfth century, chess pushed it aside, and its rules were forgotten.

Except in one place. In 1732 the young Carl Linnaeus, on his journey through Lapland, watched the Sámi play Tablut on a reindeer skin embroidered with squares, and wrote its fourteen laws into his diary. The English edition of 1811 turned "likewise the king" into "except the king", and the game historians who followed built on that slip until John C. Ashton retranslated the Latin in 2007. Today's tournament games grew out of that long reconstruction. The board here is carved after two Viking boards: the yew board from Ballinderry in Ireland, with its chevrons and pellets, and the knotted squares of the Gokstad board.
